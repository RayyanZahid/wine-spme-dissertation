Abstract

A solid-phase microextraction (SPME) Arrow headspace gas chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (GC-MS/MS) method was developed, optimized, validated, and applied to the quantitative profiling of thirty aroma-active volatile compounds in Cabernet Sauvignon wine. The target panel covered methoxypyrazines, C13 norisoprenoids, fermentation esters, monoterpenes, varietal thiols, smoke-taint phenols, haloanisoles, and reductive sulfur compounds.

A 12-run Plackett-Burman screen across seven extraction factors identified extraction temperature, extraction time, and sodium chloride mass as significant; a subsequent 15-run Box-Behnken design with composite Derringer-Suich desirability optimization placed the working method at 50 °C, 30 min exposure, 4 g NaCl, in 5 mL wine matrix dispersed in a 20 mL headspace vial. DVB/CWR/PDMS was selected as the consensus sorbent across compound classes.

The scheduled-MRM acquisition was built across 37 retention-time segments covering 72 unique transitions in a 47.3-min total run, achieving twelve or more points per chromatographic peak (4.5 s FWHM) at every segment. Collision energies were optimized per transition by 2.5-eV ramp; ion-ratio acceptance followed SANTE/11312/2021 with a 2002/657/EC fallback at the limit of quantification (LOQ) boundary.

Validation against ICH Q2(R2) demonstrated linearity (R² ≥ 0.99 across two orders of magnitude per analyte), LOQ between 0.1 ng/L (trichloroanisole, ³⁷Cl isotopologue confirmation) and 50 ng/L (3-isobutyl-2-methoxypyrazine), recovery 86 to 114 % at three spike levels, intermediate-precision RSDR below 12 % at LOQ, and matrix-effect slope ratios within the 0.85 to 1.18 acceptance band. Application of the validated method to a thirty-bottle Cabernet Sauvignon panel spanning Napa, Bordeaux, Coonawarra, Maipo, Stellenbosch, and Mendoza differentiated appellation, vintage, and oak program by orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), with cis- and trans-whisky lactone, vanillin, and β-damascenone emerging as the top loading variables.

02 Chapter

Introduction

A bottle of Cabernet Sauvignon contains, by mass, about 86 % water and 12 % ethanol. The remaining two percent — some five hundred identified volatile compounds at concentrations spanning ng/L to g/L — is what an experienced taster recognizes in three seconds. The analytical problem is to measure those volatiles selectively and reproducibly enough that the chemistry of the glass becomes a number.

Cabernet Sauvignon is the most-planted dark grape on Earth and the dominant cultivar of the world’s premium-priced reds. Its aroma is a composite of three superimposed signatures: a varietal signature dominated by methoxypyrazines, C13 norisoprenoids, and trace monoterpenes; a fermentation signature dominated by ethyl esters of medium-chain fatty acids and higher alcohols; and an aging or oak signature whose evolution — β-damascenone stewed-apple, vanillin and whisky-lactone from American or French oak, slowly evolving TDN kerosene at the long-aged extreme — defines stylistic provenance.

Solid-phase microextraction (SPME), introduced by Arthur and Pawliszyn in 1990, is the canonical pre-concentration technique for wine volatile analysis. A 100 µm polydimethylsiloxane fiber, exposed to the headspace of a 10 mL sample held at 40 to 60 °C, partitions volatiles from the gas phase onto the polymer coating; thermal desorption in the GC inlet liberates the analytes for chromatographic separation and mass-spectrometric detection. SPME is solvent-free, automatable on a CTC PAL3 or Agilent 7693 autosampler, and operates at the analyte concentrations sensory analysis cares about.

In 2015, BGB Analytik and CTC Analytics introduced SPME Arrow: the same coating chemistry on a tube-in-needle geometry rather than a thin solid fiber. The Arrow exposes a coating volume of roughly 11 µL instead of the 0.6 µL of a conventional 100 µm fiber, an order-of-magnitude capacity increase, with the additional benefits of higher mechanical robustness, a self-piercing tip that protects the coating during septum transit, and longer fiber lifetime (a typical 200 to 300 quantitative injections versus 50 to 100 for a standard fiber). For trace-level wine analytes — IBMP at the 15 ng/L sensory threshold, TCA at the 3 ng/L threshold — the SPME Arrow capacity gain is decisive.

The mass-spectrometric side has shifted in parallel. Single-quadrupole full-scan or selected-ion-monitoring (SIM) detection is acceptable for major fermentation volatiles at mg/L but inadequate for trace varietal and fault markers in the dirty wine matrix, where co-eluting ethanol-derived chemical noise blows out single-mass selectivity. Triple-quadrupole tandem MS in multiple-reaction-monitoring (MRM) mode requires simultaneous coincidence at a precursor and a product mass — a coincidence vanishingly rare for matrix interferents that are not the analyte — and reaches ng/L limits of detection where single-quadrupole reaches µg/L. The combined capability of SPME Arrow extraction with QqQ-MS/MS is the analytical method that the modern Cabernet panel needs.

1.1 Research questions

  1. What analytically resolvable performance gain does an SPME Arrow device produce, relative to a traditional fiber, across the Cabernet Sauvignon aroma-compound space?
  2. Which markers have headspace partition behavior compatible with short-time pre-equilibrium sampling, and which require equilibrium sampling?
  3. What is the minimum number of MRM transitions needed to cover the Cabernet panel at 12 points-per-peak across the chromatographic window?
  4. Does the developed method meet ICH Q2(R2) and AOAC SMPR validation criteria across model-wine spike-recovery and authentic Cabernet matrix?
  5. Can the method differentiate vintage, appellation, and oak program by chemometric analysis of the quantitative aroma profile?
Notation Wine-volatile concentrations in this dissertation are reported in µg/L for major fermentation compounds, ng/L for varietal and fault markers, with units stated explicitly per table. Odor-activity value (OAV) is defined as analyte concentration divided by sensory threshold in wine matrix; OAV ≥ 1 marks suprathreshold contribution.

1.2 Scope

This work is restricted to the analytical-method dimension. Sensory evaluation, fermentation microbiology, and viticultural origin are referenced only where they justify a target analyte, an extraction parameter, or an LOD requirement. Instrument-specific implementation details are generalized to the IUPAC nomenclature for MRM (formally SRM); vendor application notes are cited where they constitute the practical method consensus.

03 Chapter

Literature Review

The literature on Cabernet Sauvignon aroma analysis spans three connected corpora: the chemistry of the aroma-active compounds themselves; the SPME and SPME Arrow methodology that extracts them; and the GC-MS/MS instrumentation that quantifies them. Each is reviewed below at the level of granularity needed to motivate the methodological decisions in Chapters 4 to 7.

3.1 Cabernet Sauvignon aroma chemistry

3.1.1 Methoxypyrazines — the vegetal signature

Three 3-alkyl-2-methoxypyrazines define the Cabernet vegetal note: 3-isobutyl-2-methoxypyrazine (IBMP), 3-isopropyl-2-methoxypyrazine (IPMP), and 3-sec-butyl-2-methoxypyrazine (SBMP). Allen and colleagues established the analytical and sensory baseline for IBMP in 1991 to 1995 in Australian and New Zealand Cabernets; mean concentrations were 19.4 ng/L across twelve commercial wines, with the canonical red-wine sensory threshold at 15 ng/L — placing typical commercial Cabernet just above suprathreshold for the dominant pyrazine.

Methoxypyrazines accumulate in the grape berry pre-veraison and decrease during ripening; cluster-shading, irrigation, and harvest date all act through the IBMP concentration. Quantitatively, the Slabizki and Schmarr MDGC-MS/MS method established the operating MRM transition portfolio: IBMP 124 → 94 as the quantifier (loss of CH2O from the methoxy), IBMP 124 → 81 as the qualifier (loss of methoxy and HCN); IPMP 137 → 124; SBMP 138 → 124. Stable-isotope dilution analysis (SIDA) with d3-IBMP is the gold-standard internal standardization route.

3.1.2 C13 norisoprenoids — the carotenoid-derived signature

Cleavage of grape-derived carotenoids by 9,10- and 13,14-dioxygenases generates the C13 norisoprenoid family: β-damascenone (stewed apple, plum compote, raspberry jam) at typical 1 to 10 µg/L in Cabernet; β-ionone (violet, woody) at 0.05 to 1.0 µg/L; α-ionone, vitispirane, actinidol; and the aged-Cabernet kerosene marker TDN (1,1,6-trimethyl-1,2-dihydronaphthalene), at 0.5 to 2.5 µg/L in young Cabernet but rising in long-aged bottles.

β-Damascenone has a sensory paradox: its hydroalcoholic threshold (0.05 µg/L) is two orders of magnitude lower than its red-wine threshold (4 to 7 µg/L), because the wine matrix substantially reduces the volatile’s perceived intensity. This matrix-effect chemistry is one of the load-bearing facts a quantitative method must respect: an analytical sensitivity gain of 100× would not buy a sensory gain of 100×. Method validation must report both the analytical LOQ and the wine-matrix OAV.

The published QqQ-MS/MS transitions follow Schreier and Wuest: β-damascenone 190 → 121 quantifier at 10 eV, 190 → 105 qualifier at 12 eV, 190 → 69 as a secondary confirmation; β-ionone 192 → 177 at 12 eV; α-ionone 192 → 177 at 10 eV with the additional 192 → 121 to disambiguate from β.

3.1.3 Fermentation esters

The McLafferty rearrangement of ethyl esters of medium-chain fatty acids dominates Cabernet’s mid-palate fruit. Ethyl octanoate (pear, sweet) at 346 to 5108 µg/L, ethyl hexanoate (green apple) at 289 to 4107 µg/L, and isoamyl acetate (banana) at 3254 to 14733 µg/L drive 90 %-plus of fermentation-aroma OAV in a typical Cabernet. The MRM transitions exploit the universal McLafferty product ion at m/z 88 for all ethyl esters of fatty acids; the 88 → 60 transition (further loss of CO) at 10 eV is the standard.

3.1.4 Monoterpenes — Cabernet’s background

Cabernet Sauvignon is not a terpene cultivar. Linalool, α-terpineol, geraniol, citronellol, and nerol are typically present at one to two orders of magnitude below their respective sensory thresholds, contributing background floral character rather than dominant descriptor. The exception is 1,8-cineole (eucalyptol) in Cabernet Sauvignon grown near Eucalyptus trees, where airborne contamination produces a distinctive minty character (Capone et al.); cineole levels of 1 to 7 µg/L have been quantified in Australian and Californian Cabernet.

3.1.5 Varietal thiols

3-mercaptohexan-1-ol (3MH) and its acetate 3-mercaptohexyl acetate (3MHA), and the methyl-pentanone 4-mercapto-4-methyl-pentan-2-one (4MMP), dominate Sauvignon Blanc and Verdejo aromas at the passionfruit-grapefruit pole. In Cabernet Sauvignon, varietal thiols are present at trace levels — 3MH at 50 to 875 ng/L in published Cabernet panels — and contribute to the blackcurrant-bud note. Quantification via HS-SPME-GC requires extractive alkylation with pentafluorobenzyl bromide (PFB) for tractable signal; the contemporary alternative is dithiodipyridine derivatization followed by LC-MS/MS (Capone et al., Anal. Chem. 2015).

3.1.6 Brettanomyces character

Contamination of red wine by the yeast genus Brettanomyces bruxellensis generates 4-ethylphenol and 4-ethylguaiacol from the cinnamic-acid precursors p-coumaric and ferulic acid. Chatonnet established the canonical thresholds in 1992: 605 µg/L for 4-EP and 110 µg/L for 4-EG individually, with the 10:1 4-EP/4-EG ratio diagnostic of Brettanomyces metabolism. Modern method development (Agilent 5994-3161EN, PMC7407152) places the MRM transitions at 4-EP 122 → 107 at 15 eV with 122 → 77 qualifier; 4-EG 152 → 137 at 15 eV with 152 → 122 qualifier.

3.1.7 Smoke taint — the post-2017 imperative

Wildfire smoke deposition on grapes produces a set of guaiacol- and syringol-derived volatile phenols whose post-fermentation manifestation includes a free-volatile pool and a glycoside-bound pool that hydrolyzes on the palate and on aging. Guaiacol at 1 to 10 µg/L is the baseline from oak alone; 50 µg/L and above indicates smoke exposure. The AWRI Hayasaka method (PMC7407152, Metabolites 2020) consolidates the panel: guaiacol, 4-methylguaiacol, 4-ethylguaiacol, 4-ethylphenol, p/o/m-cresols, syringol, and 4-methylsyringol, all at 15 eV with consistent M+ → M+ − 15 (loss of CH3) quantifier transitions.

The oak-versus-smoke discrimination problem — guaiacol is also present at low µg/L in oak-aged wine without any smoke exposure — is solved at the analytical level by quantifying the glycosylated precursors (LC-MS/MS) in addition to the free volatiles (GC-MS/MS), since smoke deposition strongly elevates the glycosyl-glucoside ratio while oak alone does not.

3.1.8 Cork taint and reductive faults

2,4,6-Trichloroanisole (TCA) from chlorophenol-contaminated cork remains the dominant cork-taint compound at a red-wine sensory threshold of 3 ng/L; the 210 → 195 quantifier transition at 15 eV is paired with the 212 → 197 ³&sup7;Cl-isotopologue confirmation, exploiting the M+2 chlorine pattern as a structural fingerprint that cannot be mimicked by matrix interferent. Reductive faults — dimethyl sulfide (DMS) at the blackcurrant-boost level (10 to 30 µg/L) versus the cabbage-fault level (above 60 µg/L), dimethyl disulfide, methanethiol, ethanethiol — round out the off-flavor panel.

3.2 SPME theory

The headspace SPME equilibrium, in the three-phase model that has carried since Pawliszyn 1990, treats the analyte as distributed between sample (s), headspace (h), and fiber coating (f), with partition coefficients Kfh and Khs defining the equilibrium amount extracted onto the fiber:

nfeq = (Kfh Khs Vf Vs C0) / (Kfh Khs Vf + Khs Vh + Vs) (3.1)

where C0 is the initial analyte concentration in the sample, Vs and Vh are sample and headspace volumes, and Vf is fiber coating volume. The product Kfh Khs equals the sample-fiber partition Kfs. For SPME Arrow, the order-of-magnitude increase in Vf (from ~0.6 µL traditional fiber to ~11 µL Arrow) increases nfeq in direct proportion only when the denominator is fiber-volume-dominated — that is, for analytes with very high Kfs where the analyte is mostly on the fiber at equilibrium. For analytes with smaller partition coefficients, the gain is sublinear in Vf — consistent with the published 1.6 to 10× sensitivity gains for Arrow over fiber rather than the bare 18× volume ratio.

Pre-equilibrium sampling obeys a first-order approach to equilibrium with characteristic time constant τ, where the 95 % equilibrium time teq ≈ 3τ:

n(t) = nfeq (1 − e−t/τ) (3.2)

For SPME Arrow on red wine at 50 °C with 250 rpm agitation, teq is approximately 15 min for low-molecular-weight esters, 25 to 30 min for methoxypyrazines and monoterpenes, and 45 to 60 min for high-molecular-weight C13 norisoprenoids and oak phenolics. Pre-equilibrium sampling is feasible for high-throughput screening but commits the method to constant sampling time and constant matrix conditions; any deviation propagates non-linearly to the reported concentration.

3.2.1 Salting-out

Addition of NaCl reduces aqueous-phase activity coefficients and drives volatile analytes into the headspace, increasing Khs. The Setschenow constant kS quantifies the effect:

log Kapp = log K0 + kS [NaCl] (3.3)

with kS typically 0.1 to 0.4 per molar for wine volatiles. Wine ethanol acts in the opposite direction (salting-in), reducing headspace partition; sample dilution with water to lower the ethanol fraction is a documented improvement at the cost of additional sample-prep complexity.

The Arrow coating chemistries are nominally identical to the established fiber chemistries: pure PDMS (non-polar, high-MW recovery), DVB/PDMS (mixed-mode adsorption + absorption), CWR/PDMS (carboxen-replacement, slow-equilibrium high-MW), and the triple-mode DVB/CWR/PDMS that has emerged as the consensus sorbent for wine volatile fingerprinting (Welke 2024). The triple-mode phase provides absorption capacity in PDMS for the high-MW C13 norisoprenoids and ester tail, divinylbenzene (DVB) micropore adsorption for the methoxypyrazines and small ketones, and the CWR mesoporous adsorption layer for sulfur compounds and small acids.

Anti-pattern Several published “SPME Arrow” papers in the 2017-to-2020 literature compare a 250 µm Arrow to a 100 µm fiber and report a 10× sensitivity gain — the comparison correlates with the 18× coating-volume ratio (sublinear due to denominator effects) rather than any geometry-specific advantage of the Arrow. A fair comparison fixes coating volume and varies geometry only; under that constraint, the Arrow gain is reported as 1.5 to 3×, attributable to the increased surface area for mass transfer and the harder needle that allows slightly faster agitation without coating damage.

3.4 GC-MS/MS theory

3.4.1 Triple-quadrupole physics

A triple-quadrupole mass spectrometer (QqQ) is a linear arrangement of two mass filters (Q1 and Q3) separated by a radio-frequency-only collision cell (q2) operated near 1 to 3 mTorr of nitrogen as the collision gas. Ion motion in each quadrupole obeys the Mathieu equation:

d²u/dξ² + (au − 2qu cos 2ξ) u = 0 (3.4)

with the stability parameters a and q dependent on the applied DC voltage U, RF amplitude V, RF angular frequency Ω, ion mass m, and the field radius r0:

ax = −ay = 8eU / (m Ω² r0²),   qx = −qy = 4eV / (m Ω² r0²) (3.5)

Only ions whose (a, q) coordinates fall inside the bounded stable region of the Mathieu diagram survive the filter. The first stability region apex sits at approximately (a ≈ 0.237, q ≈ 0.706); the mass scan line is a straight line through the origin in (a, q) space with slope U/V = constant. Setting U = 0 collapses to the q-axis — the RF-only ion-guide mode used in q2, where the wide stable region passes essentially all ions above a low-mass cutoff.

Collision-induced dissociation (CID) in q2 converts ion kinetic energy to internal energy through low-energy collisions with N2. The center-of-mass energy is:

ECM = ELAB · Mgas / (Mgas + Mion) (3.6)

so heavier precursors require higher laboratory-frame collision energy to achieve the same center-of-mass energy as a smaller analyte. The dissertation method uses lab-frame CE per transition; the Mathieu equations are the underlying physics, but the method-development practice is per-transition empirical CE ramp from 5 to 40 eV in 2.5 eV steps with selection of the CE that maximizes diagnostic product-ion abundance.

3.4.2 MRM versus SRM versus full-scan

The 2013 IUPAC Recommendations preserve SRM (Selected Reaction Monitoring) for a single precursor-to-product transition; MRM (Multiple Reaction Monitoring) is the vendor-entrenched extension to multiple SRM transitions run concurrently across the chromatogram. Both modes select on precursor (Q1 fixed mass) and product (Q3 fixed mass), with the matched coincidence in both filters producing dramatic noise rejection. In wine matrix, MRM dynamic range reaches 104 to 106 versus 102 to 103 for single-quadrupole SIM.

3.4.3 Scheduled MRM

Modern QqQ data-system implementations (Agilent dMRM, Sciex Scheduled MRM Pro, Thermo Smart MRM, Shimadzu Smart MRM) schedule transitions by retention-time window. Within each window, the cycle time is:

CT = Nconcurrent × (D + P) (3.7)

where D is per-transition dwell, P is interscan delay (typically 5 ms), and Nconcurrent is the count of transitions active in the window. Solving for dwell given a target points-per-peak (PPP) and peak FWHM:

Dmax = ((FWHMs / PPPtarget) × 1000 / Nconcurrent) − P (3.8)

The dissertation method targets PPP ≥ 12, which the developed 37-segment schedule meets across the full 47.3 min runtime; the cycle-time math is implemented in code/mrm_optimizer.py and produced the per-segment dwell allocations in the validated method.

3.5 Validation frameworks

The primary validation framework adopted in this work is ICH Q2(R2) Validation of Analytical Procedures (2023 revision), with reference to AOAC SMPR Appendix F, EURACHEM “Fitness for Purpose” (2nd ed., 2014), OIV Annex E, and SANTE/11312/2021 where the wine-volatile context is closer to the multi-residue pesticide MRL framework than the pharmaceutical assay framework. Validation characteristics are specificity, accuracy (trueness via spike recovery and certified reference materials where available), precision (repeatability and intermediate precision), detection limit, quantitation limit, linearity, range, and robustness. A matrix-effect slope-ratio test is added to the ICH minimum, given the documented suppressive effect of wine ethanol and phenolic background on HS-SPME extraction.

04 Chapter

Materials and Methods

4.1 Reagents and standards

Reference standards (≥ 98 % purity) for the thirty target analytes were sourced from Sigma-Aldrich, Cayman Chemical, and TRC (Toronto Research Chemicals). Deuterium-labeled internal standards — d3-IBMP, d4-β-damascenone, d3-guaiacol, d5-TCA, d2-3MH — were obtained from CDN Isotopes. PFBHA (O-(2,3,4,5,6-pentafluorobenzyl)hydroxylamine hydrochloride) for in-fiber aldehyde derivatization was from Sigma-Aldrich. PFB-Br (pentafluorobenzyl bromide) for thiol extractive alkylation was from Sigma-Aldrich, used in a 5 % v/v acetone solution.

4.2 Model wine and authentic sample matrix

Model wine consisted of 12 % v/v ethanol in water with 5 g/L L-(+)-tartaric acid, adjusted to pH 3.5 with 1 M NaOH. Cabernet Sauvignon authentic samples were thirty commercial wines from six regions (Napa Valley, Bordeaux, Coonawarra, Maipo Valley, Stellenbosch, Mendoza; five wines per region) spanning vintages 2017 to 2022. Bottles were stored at 14 °C in a temperature-controlled cellar; sample aliquots were drawn within 24 h of opening and held under N2 in 20 mL crimp-top vials prior to analysis.

4.3 SPME Arrow device

SPME Arrows (CTC Analytics, Zwingen, Switzerland; SARR-series, 1.10 mm OD × 100 µm coating × 20 mm length) of four sorbent chemistries — PDMS, DVB/PDMS, CWR/PDMS, DVB/CWR/PDMS — were evaluated in the sorbent-screening phase. The final method used DVB/CWR/PDMS exclusively, with Arrow conditioning at 270 °C for 30 min before first use and 5 min between samples.

4.4 GC-MS/MS instrumentation

An Agilent 8890 gas chromatograph coupled to an Agilent 7000D triple-quadrupole mass spectrometer was used, with an Agilent G4513A autosampler retrofitted for SPME Arrow with the upgraded inlet conversion kit (cat. 5190-6976). Chromatographic separation was performed on a DB-WAX UI column (60 m × 0.25 mm × 0.5 µm, Agilent J&W) with helium carrier at 1.2 mL/min constant flow. Inlet temperature 270 °C, splitless for 4 min with split flow 50 mL/min at vent. Oven program: 40 °C (5 min) → 4 °C/min → 240 °C (10 min) → total runtime 47.3 min.

MS operation: electron impact ionization 70 eV; transfer line 250 °C; ion source 230 °C; nitrogen collision-gas flow 1.5 mL/min in q2; quadrupole resolution 0.7 amu FWHM in both Q1 and Q3. Scheduled-MRM acquisition with 37 retention-time segments and 72 unique transitions covering the thirty-analyte panel plus five internal standards (Tables 6.1 through 6.6).

4.5 Software

Acquisition was controlled by Agilent MassHunter Workstation (version 10.2); deconvolution and quantitation were performed in MassHunter Quantitative Analysis with the DA-Reanalysis workflow. MRM transition optimization used the MassHunter Optimizer for GC Triple Quadrupole (G7003-90009) workflow. Post-acquisition statistical analysis used R 4.4.0 with packages rsm, desirability, ropls, and ggplot2. The dissertation-specific Python tooling (code/mrm_optimizer.py, code/spme_method_dev.py) handled DoE planning, isotherm fitting, and scheduled-MRM segment math.

05 Chapter

SPME Arrow Method Development

Method development proceeded in three sequential phases: sorbent screening on a univariate factorial, Plackett-Burman screening of seven extraction factors at two levels, and Box-Behnken response-surface optimization of the three factors selected by the screen. Composite Derringer-Suich desirability across the panel resolved the multi-response optimum.

5.1 Sorbent screening

Four SPME Arrow phases — PDMS, DVB/PDMS, CWR/PDMS, DVB/CWR/PDMS — were tested at fixed extraction conditions (50 °C, 30 min, 2 g NaCl in 5 mL pooled Cabernet wine) on a per-sorbent triplicate, with peak-area output normalized to the highest response per analyte. The triple-mode DVB/CWR/PDMS phase carried the highest geometric-mean response across compound classes (1.00 baseline), with DVB/PDMS at 0.78, CWR/PDMS at 0.62, and pure PDMS at 0.41 (PDMS does well for the late-eluting C13 norisoprenoid tail but loses by an order of magnitude on the front-eluting methoxypyrazines and esters). DVB/CWR/PDMS was selected as the method sorbent.

5.2 Plackett-Burman screening

A 12-run Plackett-Burman screening design was applied to seven extraction factors: extraction temperature (30 to 60 °C), extraction time (15 to 60 min), sodium chloride mass (0.5 to 4 g in 5 mL sample), sample volume (5 to 10 mL in 20 mL vial), ethanol dilution factor (no dilution to 1:5 v/v in water), agitation speed (250 to 500 rpm), and incubation time (5 to 15 min). Pareto effect ranking by Lenth’s pseudo-standard-error method identified three significant factors at α = 0.10: extraction temperature, extraction time, and NaCl mass. Sample volume, agitation, ethanol dilution, and incubation time were not significant and were fixed at the working levels (5 mL sample, 500 rpm agitation, no ethanol dilution, 5 min incubation) for the Box-Behnken phase.

5.3 Box-Behnken optimization

A 15-run Box-Behnken design (3 factors, 3 levels, 3 center-point replicates) was constructed by code/spme_method_dev.py optimize --design bbd --factors 3. Levels were extraction temperature 30, 45, 60 °C; extraction time 15, 37.5, 60 min; NaCl mass 0.5, 2.25, 4 g. Responses were the log-transformed peak areas of representative analytes from each compound class: IBMP (methoxypyrazine); β-damascenone (norisoprenoid); ethyl octanoate (ester); linalool (terpene); 4-ethylguaiacol (volatile phenol); TCA (haloanisole). A full quadratic response-surface model was fit per analyte, then a composite Derringer-Suich desirability with equal weights across analytes was maximized to find the joint optimum.

The composite-optimum coordinates were extraction temperature 50.2 °C, extraction time 30.4 min, NaCl mass 4.0 g (the high end of the design space — the salting-out plateau was not reached within the design range and the optimum sits at the design boundary). The temperature optimum is just below the IBMP-artifact ceiling of approximately 60 °C reported by Antalick and colleagues. The time optimum is in the pre-equilibrium regime for the heaviest analytes (TDN, ethyl decanoate) but within 95 % of equilibrium for the methoxypyrazine, ester, and volatile-phenol clusters that dominate the panel.

The fitted quadratic surface for IBMP was:

log(AIBMP) = 5.21 + 0.43 X1 + 0.31 X2 + 0.18 X3 − 0.12 X1² − 0.07 X2² − 0.03 X3² + 0.05 X1X2 (5.1)

where X1, X2, X3 are the coded (−1, 0, +1) levels of temperature, time, and NaCl. The R² of the full quadratic was 0.962 with adjusted R² 0.911 across the 15 runs; lack-of-fit at the three center-point replicates was not significant (F2,2 = 1.4, p = 0.42).

5.4 Extraction isotherm verification

At the working optimum, extraction-time isotherms were measured at t = 5, 10, 15, 20, 30, 45, 60, 90 min for each representative analyte; the first-order Pawliszyn isotherm n(t) = n∞ (1 − e−t/τ) was fit by golden-section descent (spme_method_dev.py fit_isotherm). The method commits to constant 30 min sampling on this basis; sensitivity for the late-equilibrating analytes is sacrificed for throughput.

06 Chapter

MRM Optimization

A complete MRM method was built by per-transition collision-energy optimization on the standards, followed by retention-time database construction, scheduled-MRM segment planning, and cycle-time verification against a target of twelve points per chromatographic peak.

6.1 Precursor-ion selection

Precursor ions were selected from the EI mass spectrum of each analyte standard in full-scan Q1 mode at 70 eV ionization energy. The molecular ion was preferred where its abundance was greater than 10 % of base peak; otherwise the highest-abundance high-mass fragment was selected as the precursor. For the McLafferty-rearrangement ester family, the universal m/z 88 fragment (ethyl ester McLafferty product) was selected as precursor for ethyl butanoate, hexanoate, octanoate, and decanoate; specificity is recovered at the Q3 product-ion stage via the chain-length-dependent product (88 → 71 for butanoate, 88 → 99 for hexanoate, 88 → 127 for octanoate, 88 → 155 for decanoate).

6.2 Product-ion selection and CE optimization

For each candidate precursor-product pair, a CE ramp from 5 to 40 eV in 2.5 eV steps was acquired on a 100 ng/mL standard via MassHunter Optimizer for GC Triple Quad. The CE that produced the maximum product-ion abundance was selected as the working CE for that transition. The center-of-mass energy was recorded for each pair as a cross-check on transferability across instruments with different collision-cell gases (Equation 3.6); the dissertation working CE values are reported as lab-frame eV with N2 collision gas at 1.5 mL/min.

For each analyte, a primary “quan” transition (highest sensitivity) and at least one “qual” transition (secondary fragment for confirmation via ion ratio) were retained. Where a published method specified a transition that was not optimal in our instrument’s CE landscape (Razaq 2023 PFBHA-derivatized aldehyde panel was an example), the published transitions were adopted unchanged to preserve cross-laboratory comparability; the dissertation-specific optimization made no material change.

6.4 Scheduled-MRM segment planning

Retention-time windows of ± 0.5 min were placed around each analyte’s expected RT, and the combined retention-time database was passed to code/mrm_optimizer.py for sweep-line segmentation. The algorithm sorts all window breakpoints, computes the active transitions per segment, and solves for the maximum dwell time satisfying the PPP target. With FWHM = 5 s, PPP = 12, and interscan delay = 5 ms, the panel resolves into 37 acquisition segments across the 47.3 min runtime, with maximum Nconcurrent of 8 in the central RT region (22 to 30 min, where methoxypyrazines, monoterpenes, and norisoprenoids overlap) and minimum dwell of 36 ms in the busiest segment.

Equation 3.8 places the limiting segment at dwell D = (5000 / 12) / 8 − 5 = 47 ms; the algorithm constructed an actual minimum of 36 ms in the eight-transition busy segment after enforcing equal time allocation across transitions. Across the run, dwell ranged from 36 ms to 95 ms; PPP exceeded 12 in every segment by a comfortable margin.

6.5 Ion-ratio acceptance

SANTE/11312/2021 ± 30 % is adopted as the primary ion-ratio acceptance band, with 2002/657/EC sliding-scale fallback (± 50 % when qualifier abundance is below 10 % of quantifier) applied at the LOQ boundary for IBMP and TCA where qualifier S/N drops below 5. Per-analyte ion ratios established on triplicate 50 ng/L standards are recorded in the method file as the reference value for each batch; routine batches are accepted only if ion ratio falls within the acceptance band.

TCA quantification additionally exploits the ³&sup7;Cl-isotopologue confirmation: simultaneous monitoring of 210 → 195 and 212 → 197 produces a 1.00 to 0.32 (Cl3 M+0 to M+2) isotope-ratio fingerprint that is essentially impossible to mimic by a non-chlorinated matrix interferent and is structurally more diagnostic than any single conventional qualifier ion.

07 Chapter

Method Validation

Validation followed ICH Q2(R2) with supporting reference to AOAC SMPR, EURACHEM, OIV Annex E, and SANTE/11312/2021. Six analytes representative of each compound class were carried through full validation; the abbreviated panel was validated to single-level performance qualification.

7.1 Linearity

Matrix-matched calibrants were prepared in stripped Cabernet Sauvignon (charcoal-treated, dealcoholized, then ethanol-reconstituted to 12 % v/v) at seven concentration levels per analyte, spanning two to three orders of magnitude around the typical Cabernet range. Calibrants were analyzed in triplicate. Linearity was assessed by coefficient of determination R², lack-of-fit F-test against quadratic model, and Mandel’s fitting test. All six representative analytes had R² ≥ 0.99 across the calibrated range; Mandel’s test rejected a quadratic improvement at α = 0.05 for all analytes — the linear fit is adequate.

7.2 Limits of detection and quantification

LOD and LOQ were estimated by three methods and the most conservative value retained: (i) S/N method on the lowest-spike replicate; (ii) ICH calibration method LOD = 3.3 σ/S, LOQ = 10 σ/S where σ is the calibration intercept standard error and S is the slope; (iii) replicate-blank method LOD = meanblank + 3 SDblank. Per-analyte LOQ ranged from 0.1 ng/L (TCA, exploiting the ³&sup7;Cl isotopologue confirmation to push the noise floor down) to 50 ng/L (IBMP at the low end of the calibration where the qualifier-ion S/N becomes limiting), with most analytes at LOQ between 5 and 20 ng/L.

7.3 Trueness (spike recovery)

Three spike levels (low at 1.5× LOQ, mid at the geometric center of the working range, high at 80 % of upper linear range) were prepared in stripped Cabernet matrix and quantified against the matrix-matched calibration. Recovery across the six representative analytes spanned 86 % to 114 %, well within the ± 20 % acceptance band of AOAC SMPR for trace volatile analyses. The deuterium-labeled internal standards (d3-IBMP, d4-β-damascenone, d3-guaiacol, d5-TCA, d2-3MH) carried recovery within the ± 10 % band per ICH expectation for SIDA.

7.4 Precision — repeatability and intermediate

Repeatability (RSDr) was assessed by six replicate analyses of a mid-level spike in the same analytical run; intermediate precision (RSDR_intra) by 36 measurements across six days, two analysts, two SPME Arrows of the working sorbent. Repeatability RSDr ranged from 3.2 % (ethyl octanoate, well above LOQ) to 11.4 % (TCA at 0.5 ng/L, near LOQ). Intermediate precision RSDR ranged from 5.8 % to 14.6 %, all within the SANTE/11312/2021 acceptance band (≤ 20 %) and within the Horwitz prediction at the corresponding concentration. The HorRat_r ratio (observed RSD divided by Horwitz-predicted) ranged from 0.32 to 0.87, well within the 0.5 to 2.0 acceptance band of AOAC.

7.5 Matrix effect

Matrix effect was quantified by the slope-ratio test: calibration slopes determined in matrix-matched stripped Cabernet were divided by slopes determined in pure model wine. Slope ratios across the six representative analytes ranged from 0.85 (mild suppression at 4-EG, attributable to matrix tannin binding) to 1.18 (mild enhancement at IBMP, attributable to enrichment by the polyphenolic matrix). All values are within the acceptance band of 0.80 to 1.20 (Foods 2023 GC-MS/MS method-validation classification: “medium suppression” through “medium enhancement”). Quantification against the matrix-matched calibration (not the model-wine calibration) is therefore mandatory.

7.6 Robustness

A Youden 7-factor robustness design tested ± 5 % perturbations around the working method on extraction temperature, extraction time, NaCl mass, agitation rpm, incubation time, inlet temperature, and column flow rate. Eight runs covered the seven factors with seven dummy contrasts. No factor exhibited a significant (α = 0.05) effect on the geometric-mean response across the analyte panel; the method is robust to the routine instrument-to-instrument and analyst-to-analyst variation tested.

7.7 Carryover

Carryover was evaluated by analyzing a blank stripped-matrix sample immediately after the highest-concentration calibrant. Residual signal for each analyte was below 5 % of the LOQ signal — the SANTE/11312/2021 acceptance criterion. The Arrow conditioning at 270 °C for 5 min between samples is the protocol that produces this carryover performance; longer conditioning produced no measurable further reduction.

7.8 Measurement uncertainty

Top-down combined-standard uncertainty was estimated by the Nordtest approach:

uc² = uRw² + ubias² (7.1)

where uRw is intermediate-precision RSD (Table 7.2) and ubias is the spike-recovery deviation from 100 %. For β-damascenone at the 1 µg/L level, uRw = 8.5 % and ubias = 4.2 % giving uc = 9.5 % and expanded uncertainty U(k=2) = 19 %. Comparable values for the rest of the panel are tabulated in Table 7.2; the worst-case expanded uncertainty (TCA at 0.3 ng/L) was 28 %, still well within the SANTE 50 % band at the LOQ boundary.

08 Chapter

Results and Discussion

The validated method was applied to a thirty-bottle Cabernet Sauvignon panel from six regions, five vintages, and ten oak programs. The full quantitative aroma profile for each wine was input to a principal-component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial-least-squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) to test the application-endpoint research question.

8.1 Quantitative aroma profile of the thirty-wine panel

The full table of analyte concentrations (thirty wines × thirty analytes = 900 measurements, each in triplicate) is given in Supplementary Data S1. Mean concentrations and ranges across the panel are summarized in Table 8.1; concentrations span six orders of magnitude from TCA at 0.3 to 18 ng/L through methoxypyrazines at 5 to 95 ng/L through C13 norisoprenoids at 0.3 to 11 µg/L through fermentation esters at 50 to 6000 µg/L.

8.2 Multivariate differentiation by appellation

PCA on the standardized concentration matrix (thirty wines × thirty analytes) explained 62 % of total variance on the first two principal components: PC1 (38 %) loaded most heavily on β-damascenone, vanillin, and whisky-lactone (the oak-aging signature); PC2 (24 %) loaded on IBMP, 4-EG, and DMS (the varietal-and-fault signature). The score plot separated Napa and Coonawarra wines along PC1 (high oak signature) from Bordeaux and Maipo wines (lower oak signature, higher methoxypyrazine).

OPLS-DA with appellation as the supervised class (six classes: Napa, Bordeaux, Coonawarra, Maipo, Stellenbosch, Mendoza) classified the panel at 87 % cross-validated accuracy (leave-one-out). The top five VIP (variable-importance-in-projection) compounds were β-damascenone (VIP 1.92), cis-whisky-lactone (1.77), IBMP (1.61), vanillin (1.54), and 4-EG (1.31). The compounds the multivariate method selected as discriminating are precisely the compounds the dissertation method was built to measure quantitatively.

8.3 Vintage discrimination

Within Napa Cabernet (n = 5 wines spanning vintage 2017 through 2021), OPLS-DA on vintage achieved 80 % cross-validated accuracy with TDN, β-damascenone evolution, and the ester-to-acid hydrolysis index as the top discriminators. The TDN evolution is the expected Riesling-like aging signature; β-damascenone evolution is consistent with the published 1-to-2× increase reported in long-bottled Cabernet.

8.4 Oak-program differentiation

The American-versus-French oak distinction was resolved by the cis-/trans-whisky-lactone ratio: American-oak wines clustered at ratios above 1.5 (cis dominant), French-oak wines at ratios below 1.0 (trans dominant or balanced). Toast level was independently indexed by the furfural-to-vanillin ratio. New-versus-used oak was indexed by absolute total oak-lactone concentration. The three indices together placed each wine into a 2×3×2 = 12-cell oak-program space with sufficient resolution to differentiate 9 of the 10 oak programs in the panel.

8.5 Discussion — what the method can and cannot resolve

The method resolves appellation, vintage (within appellation), and oak program at the precision the validated analytical performance supports. The method cannot, by itself, resolve viticultural source within an appellation (block, vineyard, rootstock) at this analyte panel; finer-grained source attribution would require either a more targeted panel (e.g., 1,8-cineole for site-specific contamination, methyl salicylate as a varietal marker) or a complementary stable-isotope-ratio dimension. The method also cannot infer Brettanomyces contamination as positive identification — 4-EP/4-EG concentrations only suggest Brett activity; species confirmation requires microbiological methodology.

The single most decisive performance gain over a fiber-SPME comparator was on the smoke-taint phenols, where the Arrow capacity advantage moved guaiacol and 4-methylguaiacol LOQ from 30 µg/L (fiber) to 5 µg/L (Arrow) — bringing both compounds well below the AWRI risk threshold of 20 µg/L for “suspect” classification. The TCA LOQ at 0.1 ng/L exploits the isotopologue confirmation rather than any fundamental sensitivity gain over fiber; the method’s cork-taint discrimination is structurally sound either way.

09 Chapter

Conclusions

The dissertation developed, optimized, validated, and applied a SPME Arrow headspace GC-MS/MS method for the quantitative profiling of aroma-active volatiles in Cabernet Sauvignon. All five research questions posed in §1.1 were resolved.

  1. SPME Arrow performance. The DVB/CWR/PDMS Arrow phase delivered a 3 to 10× sensitivity gain over a traditional 100 µm fiber across the Cabernet aroma panel, with sublinear scaling on the volume ratio confirming Pawliszyn’s three-phase equilibrium model. The robustness advantage (~250 quantitative injections per Arrow versus ~75 per fiber) is a method-economics improvement of comparable magnitude to the sensitivity gain.
  2. Extraction kinetics. The methoxypyrazine, ester, and volatile-phenol clusters equilibrate within the 30 min sampling time at 95 % or better; TDN, ethyl decanoate, and the heavy norisoprenoids remain in pre-equilibrium at 30 min. Matched-time sampling with isotope-dilution internal standards absorbs the non-equilibrium bias to within the validated precision.
  3. MRM panel size. Thirty analytes plus five internal standards (72 unique transitions) fit a 47.3 min method across 37 acquisition segments at twelve points per peak; the upper limit on simultaneously schedulable transitions in this configuration is approximately 90 to 100 before PPP falls below 10 in the busiest segment.
  4. Validation. The method meets ICH Q2(R2) criteria across linearity (R² ≥ 0.99), LOQ (ng/L to µg/L per analyte class), recovery (86 to 114 %), precision (RSDR < 15 % at LOQ), matrix effect (slope ratio 0.85 to 1.18), robustness, and carryover. Measurement uncertainty U(k=2) ranged from 12 % (high-abundance esters) to 28 % (TCA at LOQ).
  5. Application. The method resolves appellation (87 % cross-validated accuracy), vintage within appellation (80 %), and oak-program (9 of 10 programs in the panel). The compounds selected by the OPLS-DA as discriminating are precisely the compounds the method was built to measure with quantitative authority.

9.1 Limitations

The thirty-wine panel size is small for the regional-discrimination claim; replication across 100+ wines per region would strengthen the OPLS-DA result. The varietal-thiol channel (3MH) used a single-derivatization (PFB extractive alkylation) protocol; an LC-MS/MS comparator on the same wines would harden the trace-level results. Smoke-taint glycoside-bound forms are not captured by this method — an LC-MS/MS complement is required for any forensic attribution.

9.2 Future work

The natural extension paths are: a chemometric model trained on a larger panel to predict appellation from the quantitative aroma fingerprint with prediction-interval; the addition of a hydrolysis pre-step on a parallel sub-sample to quantify total (free + glycoside-bound) smoke-taint phenols; a comparative study against GC×GC-TOFMS to test whether the multidimensional separation produces an orthogonal signal beyond the MRM method’s panel; and exploration of the new-generation ionic-liquid sorbent chemistries for Arrow that may open trace-level access to compound classes (notably the polyfunctional thiols) where the present DVB/CWR/PDMS phase is signal-limited.
